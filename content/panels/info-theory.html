02 · Detector ↔ Information Theory

Mapping Detector Data to Information Theory

Shannon's Key Equations were written for the channel $y = x + \eta$. A detector is that channel. Once the truth-level event is read as the transmitted message and the reconstructed event as the received one, the entropy / mutual-information / capacity machinery stops being abstract and becomes a set of statements about what an analysis can and cannot know — the same maximum-entropy spine the rest of HELIX hangs on.

The detector is a noisy channel

Identify the truth-level (particle / parton) event with the channel input $x$, the reconstructed event with the output $y$, and the detector response — resolution smearing, efficiency loss, fakes, pileup — with the channel noise $\eta$:

One caveat up front. The detector response is a migration / smearing kernel $R(y\mid x)$ — generally not additive and not Gaussian. So the discrete equations below (31, 33, 36, 46) are load-bearing; the additive-Gaussian results (52, 54) are a clean special case (good resolution, a well-measured continuous observable), not the general law.

Entropy & surprisal — eq 31, 32

$$H(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m} p(x_i)\,\log\frac{1}{p(x_i)}\ \text{bits}$$ (31)

Shannon entropy is the MaxEnt floor $H(p_k)$ made literal. At cut stage $k$, $p_k$ is the background distribution over the discriminant given only the constraints known at that stage, and its entropy is the irreducible uncertainty Jaynes says we are entitled to — and no less. The continuous form (32) is the one for displacement, $m_S$, $p_T$: differential entropy of a feature density.

Keep two uses distinct: (i) $H(p_k)$ over the background ensemble is the physics-derived bound; (ii) the predictive entropy of a classifier's $p(y\mid x)$ is the model's reported uncertainty. The HELIX epistemic-floor claim is precisely that (ii) must not fall below (i):

$$H_{\text{model}}\big(p(y\mid x)\big)\ \ge\ H(p_k)$$ floor

A Bayesian-last-layer posterior whose predictive entropy drops below the physics floor is overconfident by construction, not by bad luck.

The surprisal is the background handle. The integrand of (31), taken per event under the background model, is the surprise score:

$$S(x) = -\log p_{\mathrm{bg}}(x)$$ (surprisal)

High surprisal = anomalous = signal-like. The ABCD method is, in this language, a structured estimator of $p_{\mathrm{bg}}$ in the signal region; the surprisal is the anomaly score read off it — and it needs no signal MC .

Joint & conditional entropy — eq 33–43

Set $x$ = truth, $y$ = reconstructed observable. Then the conditional entropies split the uncertainty cleanly. The detector resolution is the noise entropy:

$$H(y\mid x) = H(\eta)\qquad\text{(detector resolution / smearing)}$$ (36)

The reverse conditional is the equivocation — residual uncertainty about truth given the measurement, $H(x\mid y)$ — and it is what actually caps unfolding. Large $H(x\mid y)$ means no procedure recovers truth cleanly; that is a fact about the data, not the algorithm. The total joint uncertainty decomposes (eq 35) and obeys the chain rule (eq 42):

$$H(x,y)=I(x,y)+H(x\mid y)+H(y\mid x),\qquad H(x,y)=H(x)+H(y\mid x)$$ (35, 42)

Marginalisation (eq 44–45) is then just forward-folding ($p(y)=\sum_x p(x,y)$, a truth spectrum pushed through the response), its inverse (unfolding), and integrating out $\eta$ / nuisances.

Mutual information — eq 46–51

The most useful group. Note that mutual information is a relative entropy — the KL divergence between the joint and the product of marginals — which is the bridge to the pyhf likelihood:

$$I(x,y)=\sum_{i,j} p(x_i,y_j)\,\log\frac{p(x_i,y_j)}{p(x_i)\,p(y_j)} \;=\; D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(p(x,y)\,\big\|\,p(x)\,p(y)\big)$$ (46)

Read against detector data, $I(\text{feature};\text{label})$ is the model-independent discriminating power of a variable: it upper-bounds the separation any classifier built on that feature can reach, so it ranks cut variables without committing to an architecture. $I(\text{embedding};\text{physical quantity})$ is exactly the WEFAT probe — "does Evt2Vec encode displacement or mass point" is an MI question, and a linear-probe AUC is a (loose) lower bound on it. The identities

$$I(x,y)=H(x)-H(x\mid y)=H(y)-H(y\mid x)$$ (49, 50)

say information gained = output entropy minus noise.

The data-processing inequality is the ceiling on the pipeline. The analysis is a cascade of channels — truth → hits → reco objects → cut variables → NN score → decision — and MI is non-increasing down the chain:

$$I(\text{truth};\text{score})\ \le\ I(\text{truth};\text{reco})\ \le\ I(\text{truth};\text{hits})$$ (DPI)

You can never recover information a stage discarded. This is the rigorous form of "the mk3/mk4 cutflow MLP cannot beat what was thrown away upstream" — and it tells you where you are bleeding: compute MI at each stage, find the biggest drop. $H(p_k)$ is the local MaxEnt floor at each link.

Channel capacity — eq 53, 54

$$C=\max_{p(x)} I(x,y)\ \ \text{bits/event}$$ (53)

Capacity is the ceiling on sensitivity: the most information about the underlying physics the detector can deliver per event, maximised over the truth distribution one could feed it. For an LLP search reduced to a presence / absence bit, $C$ bounds how well any analysis can separate hypotheses — the upstream limit on CLs reach. The Gaussian form is the clean limit for a well-measured continuous observable, with $S/N$ either resolution SNR or, in a counting region, signal-over-background:

$$C=\tfrac12\,\log\!\left(1+\frac{S}{N}\right)$$ (54)

Note its brutal logarithmic stinginess — doubling $S/N$ buys well under a bit. Sensitivity is bought in $\log$, paid for in luminosity.

Gaussian / Fourier channel — eq 52

Less central to event-level analysis, but the honest model for the other hat — detector front-end electronics, DCS / WinCC OA telemetry streams, RF instrumentation. Over bandwidth $W$ with per-frequency $S(f)/N(f)$:

$$C=\int_{0}^{W}\log\!\left(1+\frac{S(f)}{N(f)}\right)df\ \ \text{bits/s}\qquad(\text{Nyquist } 2W)$$ (52)

Synthesis

$H(p_k)$ sets the floor, capacity $C$ sets the ceiling, the DPI cascade tells you where between the two you are losing information, and a single partition function $Z$ ties the contrastive normalizer, the Bayesian evidence, and that floor together — the binding HELIX claims as its own. Same $Z$, three jobs: the embedding (InfoNCE), the background handle (surprisal), and the epistemic floor.

Equation numbering follows Stone, Information Theory: A Tutorial Introduction , §15 (Key Equations).

The one move

The detector is a noisy channel. truth $x$ → response $R(y\mid x)$ → reco $y$; the response is the noise $\eta$.

Reads as
  • Entropy → MaxEnt floor $H(p_k)$
  • Surprisal → background handle $S(x)$
  • $H(y\mid x)$ → detector resolution
  • $H(x\mid y)$ → unfolding limit
  • MI → cut-variable power, WEFAT probe
  • DPI → where sensitivity leaks
  • Capacity → ceiling on CLs reach
Caveat

$R(y\mid x)$ is a migration kernel, not additive—use the discrete eqs; Gaussian (54) is a special case.
